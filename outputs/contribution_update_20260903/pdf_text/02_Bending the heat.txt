ABSTRACT
The rapid advancement of 5G technology has significantly accelerated the progression of mobile devices, pro -
moting the evolution of electronic products such as smartphones, tablet computers, and virtual reality (VR) and 
augmented reality (AR) eyewear towards an increasingly foldable design. However, due to the inherent con -
straints of spatial and structural characteristics, conventional thermal management solutions are no longer 
adequate to meet the performance requirements of these foldable devices. Therefore, it is imperative to develop 
efficient thermal management solutions that are compatible with the cross-hinge structures within chip design. 
This study proposes and fabricates a novel ultra-thin flexible loop heat pipe (UFLHP) with a thickness of merely 
0.7 mm to address the heat transfer challenges posed by cross-hinge designs. By utilizing powder sintering and 
wire cutting techniques, an innovative approach has been developed for fabricating a metallic powder wick with 
a thickness of 0.4 mm. During the experiments, ethanol was employed as the working fluid to systematically 
investigate the effects of thermal loading, tilt angle, and bending angle on the steady-state heat transfer per -
formance of the UFLHP. The experimental results indicate that the maximum heat flux density of the UFLHP 
reaches 5 W/cm2. Under this heat flux density, the evaporator temperature of the UFLHP attains 72.15 
◦
C, while 
the thermal resistance is measured at 2.48 K/W, resulting in an effective thermal conductivity of 10,273.27 W/ 
(m ⋅ K). The tilt angle has a beneficial effect on the UFLHP ’ s performance under gravitational influence, while the 
bending angle adversely affects its performance due to increased flow resistance. This research provides a 
feasible solution for the heat dissipation challenges in foldable electronic devices.
1. Introduction
With the rapid development of mobile technology, foldable devices 
have emerged as a significant trend in the design of future electronic 
products [1] . Particularly, smartphones and wearable devices have 
gained widespread popularity due to their lightweight and multiple 
functions. As a key component of foldable devices, Augmented Reality 
(AR) and Virtual Reality (VR) equipment typically operate with power 
consumption ranging from 1 to 5 W, leading to increasingly severe heat 
dissipation challenges [2] . Excessive temperature not only affects device 
performance but also leads to a decline in user experience. Although 
existing cooling technologies such as passive cooling and fan-assisted 
cooling can address heat dissipation to some extent, their limitations 
are becoming increasingly apparent in the cross-hinge designs of fold -
able devices. Consequently, there is an urgent need for flexible and high- 
efficiency thermal management components for electronic devices.
The flexible functionality of heat dissipation components is typically 
achieved through various means, including elongated metal tubes or 
corrugated metal pipes that leverage the inherent ductility of metals [3] , 
coiled tubing that leverages material deformation capabilities [4] , as 
well as forms like organic polymer hoses [5,6] . Currently, technologies of 
flexible vapor chamber and flexible heat pipe are the mainstream in 
flexible thermal management components. Daehoon et al. [7] developed 
a metal-based flexible vapor chamber made of copper and employed the 
superhydrophilic-treated copper mesh as the capillary wick. During the 
sealing process, they used spark plasma sintering technology to mini -
mize the mechanical performance loss of the metal casing and found that 
in the unbent state it achieved a maximum effective thermal conduc -
tivity of 3,000 W/(m ⋅ K). Yu et al. [8] created a stainless steel-based 
flexible vapor chamber using brazing and laser welding techniques, 
achieving a maximum effective thermal conductivity greater than 
26,000 W/(m ⋅ K) in a horizontal position. Although flexible vapor 
chambers excelled in surface temperature uniformity, they were more 
suitable for large-area heat dissipation applications and struggled with 
long-distance heat transfer. Liu et al. [9] created a flexible heat pipe

factured a flexible heat pipe featuring an integrated bionic wick struc -
ture and the flexible polyurethane connector design. However, the 
capillary wick of the heat pipe was widely distributed throughout the 
internal structure, with gas and liquid flowing in the same channel, 
resulting in considerable flow resistance. This led to relatively weak 
performance in long-distance heat transfer and operation in reversible 
gravity environments, limiting its promotion and application.
Loop heat pipes, which consist of an evaporator, compensation 
chamber, condenser, capillary wick, vapor line, and liquid line [16 – 20] , 
also use a capillary mechanism to pump fluid. Compared to heat pipes, 
loop heat pipes are better suited for complex thermal management en -
vironments due to their separation of gas and liquid lines, high heat 
transfer coefficients, and capability for long-distance transport, making 
it a preferred heat transfer device for thermal management of electronic 
components in space vehicles [21 – 23] . In recent years, loop heat pipes 
have also gained attention for thermal management and heat transfer 
under ground conditions and have been widely applied in fields such as 
heat dissipation for high heat flux density electronic devices [21,24,25] , 
waste heat recovery [26,27] , thermal management of aircraft [28,29] , 
and solar energy utilization [30,31] . Conventional-sized loop heat pipes 
have been the subject of decades of research and application, and 
scholars have been studying the effects of various external factors (such 
as heating power, rotation angles, etc.) and internal factors (such as 
working fluid type, fill ratio, and materials and structures of compo -
nents) on the performance of the loop heat pipes [32 – 34] . In addition to 
continuously exploring and optimizing traditional loop heat pipes, 
researchers are also seeking breakthroughs in the field of micro loop 
heat pipes (mLHP) to enhance the performance and applicability of loop 
heat pipe systems. Watanabe et al. [35] developed a mLHP using a 
porous glass capillary wick, achieving a maximum heat transfer per -
formance of 15 W (60 W/cm
2
). Shioga et al. [36] developed a mLHP 
using a chemical etching and diffusion bonding process with thin copper 
plates. Experimental results showed that under a thermal input of 20 W, 
the thermal resistance between the evaporator and condenser reached 
0.11 K/W, and the operational direction had minimal impact on the 
mLHP. Takuji et al. [37] designed a 0.3 mm-thick mLHP for cooling 
mobile devices, demonstrating a maximum heat transfer performance of 
10 W/cm
2
, with a minimum thermal resistance and effective thermal 
conductivity of 2.51 K/W and 2,438 W/(m ⋅ K). Notably, all the above- 
mentioned mLHP featured rigid structures, and there have not yet 
been applications or related technical patents for ultra-thin flexible loop 
heat pipe (UFLHP). Research on conventional-sized flexible loop heat 
pipes remains relatively limited. Xu et al. [38] effectively achieved 
flexible functionality in loop heat pipe (thickness: 41 mm) by combining 
a stainless steel braided liner with a smooth PTFE metal hose, resulting 
in a vapor line length of nearly 5 m and a maximum heat transfer rate 
exceeding 700 W. Additionally, Lee et al. [39] introduced metal bellows 
in the vapor – liquid lines of loop heat pipe (thickness: 31 mm) to achieve 
flexible functionality. Under conditions using water as the working 
fluid, their study demonstrated a minimum thermal resistance of 0.25 K/ 
W at a heat load of 320 W.
This study presents a novel ultra-thin flexible loop heat pipe struc -
ture (UFLHP) with a thickness of 0.7 mm, alongside the development of 
an innovative ultra-thin metal powder wick measuring 0.4 mm, ach -
ieved through powder sintering and wire cutting technology. By 
replacing the gas – liquid line of the loop heat pipe with flexible tubing 
made from polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), the design incorporates 
woven copper wire treated with oxalic acid in the liquid line as a sec -
ondary capillary structure to facilitate liquid return. The proposed 
UFLHP design embodies two key innovations: it synergistically com -
bines the excellent thermal conductivity of metallic materials with the 
flexibility of PTFE, thereby significantly enhancing thermal perfor -
mance while ensuring structural adaptability under varying environ -
mental conditions. Furthermore, the study introduces a novel 
trapezoidal serrated capillary wick design that allows for the integration 
of vapor and liquid pathways on the same side of the evaporator. This 
optimization not only effectively reduces the overall size of the device 
but also showcases substantial potential in applications requiring high 
thermal performance and spatial integration, particularly in augmented

2. Experimental apparatus
2.1. Experimental rig
Currently, UFLHP is designed and manufactured based on AR de -
vices. Since the main control chips of most AR devices are located on the 
side of the temples, the evaporator end of the UFLHP is positioned on the 
temple side, as shown in Fig. 1 . The vapor and liquid channels pass 
through the hinge structure, while the condenser end is placed at the 
bridge position and cooled by natural air convection. Fig. 2 (a) illustrates 
the structural schematic of the UFLHP and the locations of the temper -
ature measurement points. The UFLHP consists of an evaporator, 
condenser, vapor line, liquid line, capillary wick, and secondary capil -
lary wick. Compared to traditional loop heat pipes, the UFLHP features 
an improvement in the evaporator with a gas – liquid co-planar design, 
integrating the original compensation chamber structure into the liquid 
line to reduce thickness. Under known heat input conditions, the heat 
transfer performance of the UFLHP is evaluated by measuring the tem -
peratures at various points. In the experiment, seven K-type thermo -
couples were connected to the surface of the UFLHP: the center of the 
evaporator (Evap), the outlet of the evaporator (Evap-out), different 
sections of the condenser (C-1, C-2, C-3, C-4), and the return section of 
the evaporator (Evap-in). A thermocouple with the accuracy of ± 0.5 K 
monitors the ambient temperature. The evaporator is constructed from 
high-strength brass, while the capillary wick is made of copper. The 
cover plate, capillary wick, and bottom plate are welded together by 
using a diffusion welder, with the internal structure illustrated in Fig. 2
(b). To ensure stability, bendability, and high gas tightness, flexible 
material for the vapor and liquid line is selected to be polytetrafluoro -
ethylene (PTFE), as shown in Fig. 2 (c). The seal between the evaporator 
and the PTFE tubing is achieved by using 3 M ’ s DP420 epoxy AB glue. 
The basic parameters and material information relevant to the experi -
ment are presented in Table 1 . During the experiment, a ceramic heater 
measuring 10 mm × 10 mm was used to provide heat load to the 
evaporator, with different heating conditions simulated by adjusting the 
output current of the DC power supply.
The capillary forces generated by the capillary wick serve as a 
driving force for the loop heat pipe. In the UFLHP, the capillary wick is 
divided into a primary capillary wick and secondary capillary wick. The 
former is made from 100 µ m brass powder and is produced through four 
steps: molding, sintering, cleaning, and wire-electrode cutting, as shown 
in Fig. 3 . The ultra-thin thickness of the metal powder wick of only 0.4 
mm is difficult to achieve in a single sintering, so a large capillary wick is 
first sintered and then cut by using a wire electric discharge machine to 
obtain the desired wick structure. The upper part of the main capillary 
wick is designed in a rectangular shape to facilitate gas – liquid separa -
tion, while the lower part has a serrated structure that serves as the 
vapor channel. The secondary capillary wick is positioned along the 
liquid line and is welded to the mian capillary wick, as illustrated in 
Fig. 2 (b). This secondary capillary wick exhibits strong deformability, 
ensuring that its placement within the pipe does not hinder the flexi -
bility characteristics of the UFLHP. The design of the secondary capillary 
wick aims to provide additional capillary driving force for the UFLHP, 
facilitating a more rapid flow of the working fluid toward the main 
capillary wick during the condensation process, owing to the capillary 
action of the secondary capillary wick. Moreover, the secondary capil -
lary wick possesses a certain liquid storage capacity, effectively pre -
venting the primary capillary wick from drying out. The secondary 
capillary wick is constructed from spiral woven copper wire with a mesh 
count of 14 and a porosity of 68.9 %, as shown in Fig. 4 . This material is 
placed in a mixture of 300 ml of water and 10 g of oxalic acid, then 
heated in a 50 
◦
C water bath for treatment, followed by 30 min of ul -
trasonic cleaning, a thorough rinse with clean water, and final vacuum- 
drying.
In the experimental setup, a rotating platform testing UFLHP gravity 
performance was employed, in which the entire UFLHP testing appa -
ratus was mounted. In this experimental configuration, the tilt angle 
between the evaporator and the horizontal plane was denoted as α
◦
, as 
illustrated in Fig. 5 (a). To investigate the impact of bending perfor -
mance on the UFLHP, the bending angle between the plane of the 
evaporator and that of the condenser was defined as β
◦
, as depicted in 
Fig. 5 (b). Initially, primary vacuum was achieved by using a mechanical 
pump, followed by secondary vacuum facilitated by a molecular pump. 
Once the pressure was reduced to below 0.1 Pa, the evacuation valve 
was closed, and the liquid injection valve was opened. Subsequently, the 
working fluid was injected, with ethanol selected as the working me -
dium, and the filling ratio maintained at 30 %. Upon completion of the 
injection process, the injection port was severed and sealed simulta -
neously by using a tube welding apparatus. The cooling method design 
employed natural air cooling technology, with the environment tem -
perature regulated at 23 
◦
C through an air conditioning system to ach -
ieve an effective natural air cooling process. To ascertain the thermal 
performance of the UFLHP, several sets of thermal performance exper -
iments were conducted, as detailed in Table 2 .

The practical application of UFLHP primarily focuses on AR and VR 
devices, where manufacturing precision is crucial. The evaporator and 
capillary wick are fabricated using diffusion bonding and wire-cutting 
techniques, and their dimensional accuracy relies on the precision of

The assessment of performance and industrial application of UFLHP 
necessitates a thorough inquiry into their startup characteristics. Fig. 8

increase in the evaporator outlet temperature T
Evap-out

Fig. 9 (a) illustrates the time required for the startup process of the

required for Stage I is primarily attributed to the high vacuum level 
within the UFLHP, which results in a reduced influence of gravity on gas 
flow. In this case, the gas will follow the laws of hydrodynamics and 
move along the tilt direction of the pipe, making it easier to discharge 
upwards. As the degree of tilt angle increases, the vapor is more readily 
expelled from the evaporator, thereby shortening the time required for 
Stage I. The variation in the time required for Stage II is primarily due to 
the following three factors: First, the increase in the tilt angle α allows 
the vapor to reach the condenser more quickly for condensation. Second, 
once the vapor has condensed into liquid in the condenser, it is more 
significantly influenced by gravity. Finally, the increase in the tilt angle 
α enables the working fluid to make contact with the secondary capillary 
wick earlier, promoting the return flow of the liquid to the evaporator 
through capillary action. The variation in the time required for Stage III 
is primarily due to the relatively short durations of the first two stages, 
which results in a smaller temperature increase at the evaporator inlet 
T
Evap-in 
at the beginning of the cycle establishment. This consequently 
shortens the time required to reach the equilibrium temperature.

4. Conclusions
This study proposes and fabricates a novel ultra-thin (thickness of 
0.7 mm) flexible loop heat pipe (UFLHP) to address the heat transfer 
issues associated with crossed hinges. The research focuses on the 
impact of the tilt angle and bending angle of the UFLHP on its thermal 
performance, leading to the following conclusions:
The UFLHP uses flexible polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) for the 
vapor and liquid lines, accommodating a maximum thermal input of 5 
W. At this input, the evaporator temperature reaches 72.15 
◦
C, with a 
thermal resistance of 2.48 K/W and an equivalent thermal conductivity 
of 10273.27 W/(m ⋅ K).
The UFLHP exhibits improved heat transfer performance with an 
increasing tilt angle α , reaching optimal conditions at 90
◦
and a heat 
load of 4 W, yielding an evaporator temperature of 63.16 
◦
C, thermal 
resistance of 2.81 K/W, and equivalent thermal conductivity of 
9,072.85 W/(m ⋅ K). In contrast, performance declines as the bending 
angle β increases; at 90
◦
, the evaporator temperature rises to 66.49 
◦
C, 
thermal resistance increases by 75.96 % to 4.94 K/W, and equivalent 
thermal conductivity decreases by 43.17 % to 5,156.12 W/(m ⋅ K).
Under the same conditions, the UFLHP performs better during hot 
starts than cold starts, with a 1.88 
◦
C lower evaporator temperature. 
These findings are vital for optimizing ultra-thin flexible loop heat pipes, 
particularly regarding tilt angle, bending angle, and startup conditions 
in practical applications.
