ABSTRACT
With the rapid development of microelectronics technology and the increasing heat flux of electronic devices,
loop heat pipes have garnered significant attention for their highly efficient passive heat transfer performance.
However, the heat transfer performance of conventional loop heat pipes is impaired by the heat leakage, and the
maximum heat flux is unable to meet the heat dissipation requirement of high-heat-flux devices. To solve this
problem, a loop heat pipe with a vapor-driven injector (LHPI) was previously proposed. However, only the heat
transfer performance of the horizontally-placed LHPI had been investigated in the preliminary stage, and the
effect of gravity on the heat transfer performance was not further discussed. Now in this paper, a 360
◦
rotatable
experimental bench is designed to investigate the influence of the tilt angle on the performance of LHPI. In
addition, a capillary wick is integrally sintered with the trough baseplate and the microcolumn baseplate of the
LHPI, respectively, to reduce the thermal resistance between them. The results show that the heat transfer
performance of LHPI is significantly affected by its tilt angle. In the horizontal condition, LHPI exhibits the lowest
baseplate temperature and thermal resistance, and the best heat transfer performance, which verifies its appli-
cations in engineering. Integral sintering increases the maximum thermal load from 300 W to 350 W under stable
operation conditions of LHPI. The maximum thermal load of the integrated micro pin-finned baseplate reaches
500 W, which improves the heat dissipation effect by 42 % in heat dissipation compared to the trough baseplate
under the same conditions., providing an improvement angle for the traditional LHP.
1. Introduction
As for the Loop Heat Pipe (LHP), a passive phase change heat transfer
device, the evaporator liquid, driven by the capillary force generated
from the capillary wick, absorbs heat, undergoes phase change, enters
the condenser through the vapor line, and finally returns to the
compensation chamber through the liquid line under the action of
capillary force to complete the transfer of heat as shown in Fig. 1 a.
Compared with the traditional heat pipe, LHPs have advantages such as
strong heat transfer performance, a long heat transfer distance, and a
flexible arrangement. Since the 1980 s, LHPs have been the heat transfer
device of choice for thermal management of electronic components in
space vehicles. In recent years, it has also received extensive attention
for thermal management and heat transfer under ground conditions,
such as heat dissipation in computer CPUs [1 – 4] and high-power LEDs
[5] , solar thermal utilization [6] , and aircraft de-icing [7] .
The achievement of self-circulation of the working fluid is considered
a key problem in the research field of loop heat pipes. Conventional
LHPs use the capillary force generated by the capillary wick as the
driving source for circulation. Therefore, in the design process, it is
generally based on the principle that the capillary force( Δ P

mass transfer processes within the loop, resulting in different start-up
and steady-state operating characteristics of the LHP. When the

capillary forces directly affect the maximum heat transfer capacity, heat

In the current time, the optimization of the working fluid circulation
of LHP focuses on two aspects: enhancing capillary force and reducing
pipeline resistance. According to the Laplace-Young equation, the
smaller the effective pore size of the capillary wick is, the greater the
capillary force is. However, there is a contradictory relationship be-
tween capillary force and resistance. Sintered metal powder capillary
wicks have pore sizes of a few micrometers to tens of micrometers [8] ,
which have a high capillary force and a high resistance for the working
fluid flow; metal mesh and metal foam capillary wicks have a large pore
size and their porosity is up to 90 % or more, in addition to low resis-
tance for working substance flow and low capillary forces [9] . Xu et al.
[10] prepared a double-pore capillary wick by mixing and sintering with
a pore-making agent and metal powder, which solved the problem of
high flow resistance and effectively improved the performance of LHP.
In terms of pipeline resistance, it has been found that in experiments
with water as the working fluid, steam vapor line pressure drops account
for about 50 % to 80 % of the total pressure drop [11,12] . Maydanik
et al. [13] decreased the pressure drop by increasing the diameter of the
vapor line to achieve a maximum heat load of 1,174 W for a flat-plate
type LHP. However, as the heating power increased, the pressure drop
in the line escalated rapidly until the capillary force was no longer able
to overcome the resistance to flow and replenish liquid for evaporator,
leading to a start-up failure known as the capillary limit, which is often
reached before the boiling limit [14] and meanwhile plays a crucial role
in influencing the performance of LHPs [15] . It was shown that the
critical heat flow density of liquid film evaporation or boiling within the
capillary wick of sintered metal powder under a negative pressure
environment was as high as 990 W/cm
2
with sufficient replenishment
[16] , which was much higher than that of the conventional LHPs [17] .
The results by Lee and Park [14] and Wang et al. [18] have shown that
the main reason for the startup failure of LHPs at high heating power is
the insufficient liquid recharge that causes the dryout of the evaporator
baseplate, rather than reaching the boiling limit.
While optimizing the capillary wick structure and the vapor – liquid
line can enhance the heat transfer performance of the LHP to some
extent, it does not fundamentally resolve the limitations of the capillary
limit. Therefore, a LHP with the assistance of an electrically driven
micropump has been proposed. Lee and Park [16] discovered that
micropumps could enhance liquid flow, and theoretically, the heat flow
density of LHP could reach 259.8 W/cm
2
, which is significantly higher
than that of a conventional LHP. They also pointed out that further
improvement could be achieved by optimizing the capillary structure.
Jiang et al. [19] solved the problem of temperature fluctuation and start-
up failure of LHPs by using micropumps. Moreover, several researchers
have noted that placing the evaporation section below the condenser
allows gravity to assist in liquid reflux, thereby enhancing the heat
transfer efficiency of LHPs [20 – 23] . Conversely, when the evaporator is
situated above the condenser, gravity acts as a hindrance to liquid
reflux, leading to a significant decrease in the heat transfer efficiency of
LHPs. This is evidenced by the notable increase in the temperature of the
evaporator baseplate under the same heating power [22,24 – 27] .
It can be seen that it is effective to enhance the liquid working sub-
stance flow by adding an additional circulation drive, which leads to a
significant improvement in the heat transfer performance of conven-
tional LHPs. However, the use of micropumps in LHPs requires an
additional power source, which increases the complexity of the system
and reduces the LHP ’ s reliability. While gravity can assist in the return of
fluids, it severely limits the occasions on which the LHP can be used. In
2015, DuPont et al. [28,29] proposed a capillary jet loop heat pipe (CJL)
technology that integrates an injection pump with a loop heat pipe. The
injection pump, serving as a passive device, utilizes steam generated
through evaporation and boiling to inject the working fluid into the
compensation chamber, thereby enhancing the heat transfer perfor-
mance of the loop heat pipe. The study reported by Kiseev and Sazhin
[30] regarding CJL utilizing distilled water as the working fluid
demonstrated that the incorporation of an injector condenser can be
used in cooling systems with increased heat transfer distance. Clavenna
et al. [31] investigated the performance of the CJL in horizontal and
vertical orientations under 0 to 1.8 g gravity conditions. The results
showed that the device could operate in a stationary state under
hypergravity and normal gravity conditions when the power exceeded
100 W. In our previous work, a loop heat pipe driven by a vapor-driven
jet injector (VDJI) namely LHPI was proposed [32] . The feasibility of the
LHPI was experimentally verified under a wide range of heating power.
LHPI was a device that improves upon CJL in two aspects. Firstly, a
secondary condenser was added in the liquid line III to lower the tem-
perature T
L-noz
or subcooling of the injected liquid into the VDJI, which
enhanced the condensation potential between vapor and liquid inside
the mixing chamber of the VDJI and contributed to the enhancement of
the performance of the VDJI [33] . Secondly, the vapor nozzle used in the
VDJI of the LHPI was a Laval nozzle with convergent-divergent channel,
which allowed the secondary expansion of the vapor and created a lower
pressure in the nozzle exit, thereby enhancing the performance of the
VDJI. On the whole, the LHPI showed a improvement in heating power
compared to the CJL, and exhibited higher stability . The vapor passes
through a specially designed shrinking and a discharging flow channel,

For LHPI, when there is a height difference between the evaporator

2. Experimental apparatus
2.1. Experimental rig
Fig. 1 b shows the structure of the LHPI and the distribution of the
temperature measurement points. LHPI mainly consists of evaporator,
VDJI, condenser I, condenser II, vapor lines, and liquid lines, with a
certain amount of deionized water introduced into the interior of the
LHPI by the evacuation-filling process, and the filling ratio set at 70 % in
this paper.
Evaporator: The key component of the LHPI, the built-in capillary
wick divides the internal space into three parts: the compensation
chamber, the capillary wick and the vapour channel. The compensation
chamber is equipped with a return hole and a pilot hole. The liquid from
the outlet of condenser I is directed back to the compensation chamber
through liquid line II, while the pilot hole is connected to the liquid line
of VDJI. Some of the liquid enters the vapor channel by capillary force to
evaporate and dissipate heat, while the rest flows into liquid line II. The
capillary wick is a porous structure made of sintered copper powder,
serving two main purposes: (1) providing capillary force to pump liq-
uids, and (2) isolating vapor from entering the compensation chamber.
VDJI: The structure and working principle of VDJI is shown in Fig. 2 .
The VDJI consists of a vapour nozzle (Laval structure), a water nozzle, a
mixing chamber and a diffusion section. At high heating power level,
vapor from the evaporator enters the steam vapor nozzle of the VDJI to
expand and accelerate, reaching the sound speed in the steam vapor
nozzle throat, and then expands to supersonic speed at the exit, creating
a lower negative pressure zone within the mixing chamber. This lower
pressure ejects the liquid from the compensation chamber into the
mixing chamber, where it mixes with the high-speed vapour through
Liquid Pipeline II. The resulting high-speed vapor – liquid two-phase flow
near the throat of the mixing chamber leads to condensation surge,
causing a sudden pressure rise. This process converts the internal energy
of the vapour into the pressure energy of the liquid.
Condenser I: The main condenser designed as a casing structure
primarily operates to cool the hot fluid circulating inside the tube using
cold water on the outside of the casing. This process lowers the tem-
perature of the hot fluid at the outlet of the VDJI, removes heat, and
helps maintain the energy balance of the LHPI.
Condenser II: The subcondenser also constructed as a casing utilizes
cooling water on the outside of the casing to cool down the induced
fluid. This process enhances the subcooling of the liquid entering the
VDJI, thereby improving the performance of the VDJI [34] .
The cooling water lines of condenser I and condenser II are con-
nected in series, and the external circulating coolant is supplied by a
water cooler with a controlled outlet temperature (known as the heat
sink temperature) set at 10
◦
C for this experiment.

3.2. Effect of tilt angle on start-up characteristics
The startup characteristics are crucial for evaluating the performance
of the LHPI. Condensers I and II are activated before the thermal input in
the LHPI system. For Liquid Line III, the internal liquid state is influ-
enced by both gravity and the injection capability of the injector. When
β > 0
◦
, a portion of the liquid is present in Liquid Line III due to the effect
of gravity. The startup process of the LHP under operating conditions
with a thermal load of 200 W is illustrated in Fig. 7 , and the startup
process can be broken down into three stages:
Stage I: the vapor generation stage. After applying a thermal load,
the working fluid in the evaporator is in a subcooled state, while
absorbing heat and evaporating, eventually reaching the evaporator
outlet. The conclusion of this stage is indicated by a sharp rise in the
evaporator outlet temperature ( T
Vap
).
Stage II: the liquid eject stage. Initially, there is not enough vapor to
activate the VDJI. As vapor accumulates to a certain level, it enters
the injector, causing the rise of the temperature of the liquid nozzle
( T
L-noz
) (VDJI acts like a pipeline). As the evaporator temperature
increases, the vapor volume rises, triggering the VDJI induction ef-
fect. This causes the liquid in the compensation chamber to enter the
VDJI, mixing with and condensing the steam. Subsequently, a
condensation surge forms in the diffusion section, leading to a
boosting effect that propels the liquid towards condenser I. The
conclusion of this phase is indicated by a sharp decrease in the liquid
nozzle temperature ( T
L-noz
).
Stage III: the cycle establishment stage. With the continued increase
in evaporator temperature, vapor production also increases. With the
successful ejection of the VDJI, the liquid at the VDJI outlet has
enough pressure to pass through condenser I and return to the
compensation chamber, completing one cycle of the LHPI. The
conclusion of this stage is indicated by a decrease in evaporator
temperature ( T
Evap
) and evaporator outlet temperature ( T
Vap
).
