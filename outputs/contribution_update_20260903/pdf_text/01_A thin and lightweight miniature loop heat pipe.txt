INTRODUCTION
Electronic devices are constantly evolving toward higher perfor -
mance, lighter weight, and greater integration, which increases 
the demand for more effective thermal management.
1
Currently, 
most commercial mobile devices, including smartphones, tab -
lets, and VR headsets, use graphite heat spreaders for cooling.
2
However, the heat dissipation performance of these materials is 
gradually falling short of current requirements, and there is a 
need to explore alternative technologies to address the existing 
limitations of graphite heat spreaders. Phase-change heat-dissi -
pation technologies, due to their thermal performance, have 
emerged as robust candidates for advanced thermal manage -
ment, particularly phase-change materials (PCMs) and vapor 
chamber (VC) and heat pipe (HP) technologies.
3
PCMs absorb 
heat during the solid-liquid phase change and can respond to 
temperature fluctuations, maintaining a relatively stable temper -
ature.
4
They have been used in battery
5
and printed circuit 
board
6
cooling. However, PCMs face challenges such as 
leakage risks during phase transition, low thermal conductivity, 
and poor secondary heat-dissipation performance, which limit 
their range of applications.
7
In contrast, both VC and HP technol -
ogies absorb and dissipate heat through liquid-vapor phase 
change and achieve thin designs through flattening, addressing 
the thermal dissipation needs of compact electronic devices.
8
VC technology focuses on uniform heat dissipation over large 
surface areas,
9–11
while HPs have advantages in directional 
heat transfer.
12–16
However, both technologies fail to achieve 
proper gas-liquid separation, and at high power levels vapor 
diffusion can carry the returning liquid, limiting heat-transfer dis -
tance and equivalent thermal conductivity. Moreover, in small 
electronic devices such as smartphones, further thinning and

Loop heat pipes (LHPs), an extension of traditional HP tech -
nology, utilize a capillary pumping mechanism for fluid transport. 
The system comprises an evaporator, compensation chamber, 
wick, vapor line, condenser, and liquid line.
17–21
LHPs separate 
vapor and liquid flow paths through an independent wick, effec -
tively mitigating the carry-over effect inherent in conventional 
HPs. This configuration endows LHPs with advantages such 
as rapid startup, flexible layout, long heat-transfer distances, 
and high heat-transport capacity. The development of LHP tech -
nology can be traced back to 1972 when researchers first 
explored the use of capillary-driven two-phase fluid.
22
Following 
its in-orbit validation on the Granat satellite in 1989, LHPs have 
become a common thermal management solution for spacecraft 
due to their passive self-circulation capability and high heat-load 
adaptability.
23
LHPs have also been used for terrestrial applica -
tions,
24
including high-power electronic device cooling,
25–28
so -
lar energy utilization,
29 , 30
and waste heat recovery.
31
To address the thermal management requirements of light -
weight and highly integrated mobile electronic devices, re -
searchers are developing miniature loop heat pipes (mLHPs) 
to enhance system capacity and expand their applicability. 
Shioga et al.
32
fabricated an mLHP using chemical etching 
and diffusion bonding techniques, achieving a thermal resis -
tance of 0.11 K/W under a 20-W heat load. Mizutani et al.
33

W/cm
2
) mobile devices, demonstrating a maximum heat flux 
of 10 W/cm
2

an effective thermal conductivity of 2438 W/m ⋅ K. Kirshberg 
et al.
34
fabricated a silicon-based mLHP (1 × 1 mm
2
) via photo -
lithography and bonding processes, integrating vapor-liquid 
channels into a high-purity silicon substrate and sealing 
the evaporator, flow paths, and condenser with glass. Hsu

35

tion limit of 12.96 W and a minimum operational thermal resis -
tance of 0.106
◦

36

less-steel mLHP using an anhydrous polytetrafluoroethylene 
(PTFE) porous membrane as the wick, achieving a heat-transfer 
performance of 11 W under natural air cooling. Detailed exper -
imental performance metrics of the selected published mLHPs, 
including thickness, heat flux, and range of evaporator temper -
ature, are summarized in Table S1 . Additionally, co-planar lay -

thickness reduction in mLHPs.
36 , 37
However, the opposing-

In this work, we present an mLHP that is thin (0.7 mm) and 
lightweight (3.95 g). Our prior research eliminated the traditional 
compensation chamber and incorporated a stepped-serrated 
main wick structure integrated with a braided copper wire sup -
plementary wick to enhance fluid return.
38
Building on this foun -
dation, we use a nickel fiber mat as the main wick, with a copper 
evaporator, polymer-based vapor-liquid lines, and a condenser, 
all assembled through a solder-free bonding technique. System -
atic experimental investigations evaluate the liquid absorption 
performance of the nickel fiber mat wick under varying heating 
powers, tilt angles, and long-term operation, along with the 
heat-transfer properties of the mLHP.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Device design and operating principle
The mLHP consists of the following components: an evapo -
rator, main wick, supplementary wick, condenser, vapor line, 
and liquid line, as shown in Figure 1 . The thermal transfer effi -
ciency of the mLHP is evaluated under specified thermal input 
circumstances by recording temperatures at various locations. 
Throughout the experiment, temperatures were recorded at 
eight different locations, seven of which were on the mLHP’s 
surface, using thermocouples with a 0.5-K precision. The spe -
cific locations measured include the center of the evaporator, 
the outlet and the inlet of the evaporator, and condenser sec -
tions (i.e., C-1 to C-4, as shown in Figure 1 A). One measure -
ment point was placed in the environment to monitor the 
ambient temperature. In the experiment, various input power 
conditions were simulated by altering the output current of 
the electricity source, and a ceramic heating plate with dimen -
sions of 10 × 10 mm
2 
was used to deliver input power. The 
fundamental parameters and material details used in the exper -
iment are provided in Tables 3 , 4 , and 5 .
The capillary force generated by the wick is the driving force 
behind the functioning of the mLHP, and its performance af -
fects the overall performance of the mLHP. The wicks of the 
mLHP are divided into two categories: main and supplemen -
tary. The main wick uses a nickel fiber mat as the base mate -
rial, with a thickness of 0.4 mm, and is shaped using wire-cut -
ting technology. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
image of this is presented in Figure 2 A. The preparation pro -
cess of the nickel fiber mat involves drawing nickel wire into 
long, thin fibers, cutting them, laying them down using an air 
flow, and finally sintering to form the mat. Compared to tradi -
tional metal powder sintered wicks, the nickel fiber mat wick 
exhibits higher toughness and connection strength. Even after 
undergoing bending deformation, its appearance and capillary 
performance remain unchanged. The pore size of the nickel fi -
ber felt wick follows a normal distribution due to the random 
arrangement of the nickel fibers and can be broadly classified 
into two types: oversized pores and effective pores. The effec -
tive pores provide the necessary capillary force for capillary 
action, while the oversized pores minimize the flow resistance. 
Additionally, during the preparation process, the nickel fiber 
mat demonstrates low sensitivity to various influencing fac -
tors, ensuring better consistency in its performance. These 
characteristics give the nickel fiber mat wick advantages in 
practical applications. The main wick consists of two layers: 
the upper layer, which is rectangular and designed for va -
por-liquid separation, and the lower layer, which features a 
serrated design to function as the vapor escape route. The 
size of the serrated structure in the lower layer follows a 
decreasing trend, which enables the vapor-liquid flow paths 
to be concentrated on the same side, thus effectively reducing 
the overall width of the mLHP. The supplementary wick, con -
structed from woven spiral copper wire, leverages the surface 
tension of the liquid to provide initial liquid storage and

Cooling performance during heating
Transient operating characteristics are crucial for analyzing dy -
namic responsiveness, optimizing design, and diagnosing de -
vice defects. The startup characteristics under the input power 
of Q = 1 W are illustrated. The startup process has three key 
stages, as illustrated in Figure 3 A. Stage I is marked by the rise 
in vapor temperature ( T
Evap-out
). After the DC power supply is 
turned on, heat is conducted from the ceramic heating plate to 
the evaporator through thermal grease, causing a rise in the 
evaporator temperature ( T
Evap
). The working fluid inside the 
evaporator transitions from subcooled to saturated state, and 
vapor is generated as part of the heat-absorption process. Stage 
II is completed when the evaporator inlet temperature ( T
Evap-in
) 
starts to decrease. During this stage, although heat is conducted 
into the evaporator, the mLHP cycle has not yet formed, so the 
evaporator inlet temperature increases due to the heat-conduc -
tion effect of the baseplate. Stage III is marked by the system 
temperature gradually stabilizing. At this stage, a small amount 
of working fluid flows sequentially through the evaporator outlet 
and various points on the condenser (C-1 to C-4), returning to the 
evaporator inlet, which causes the evaporator inlet temperature 
to drop once the cycle is established. Stage III is marked by the 
system temperature gradually stabilizing. In this stage, enough 
liquid working fluid is in the cycle to establish circulation.

the establishment of the mLHP cycle. Therefore, gravity plays a 
negative role in this process. As the input power increases, the

Long-term durability under high-temperature aging 
tests
Traditional LHPs are prone to a decrease in sealing perfor -
mance over extended operation periods, leading to an increase 
in the amount of non-condensable gas inside, which in turn re -
sults in a decline in their heat-transfer performance. Addition -
ally, organic materials often exhibit poor gas-barrier properties 
due to their molecular polarity, crystallinity, and other charac -
teristics, which may cause performance degradation in assem -
bled products after long-term operation. In this study, the 
mLHP device was placed in an oven at 90
◦
C to undergo 
high-temperature aging tests. The mLHP was removed for per -
formance testing every 5 days, and the test results are shown in 
Figure 6 A, with no observable performance degradation after

Conclusions
In this work, we present a thin and lightweight mLHP with nickel 
fiber felt as the wick and a copper-plated polyimide film as the 
condenser. During the fabrication process, welding was per -
formed without the use of solder, achieving laboratory-scale 
mass production. Compared to other commonly used metal 
wicks in ultra-thin LHPs, nickel fiber felt demonstrates stronger 
capillary force and higher fabrication stability and scalability. 
As the input power increases ( Q
in 
> 3 W), the thermal perfor -
mance of the mLHP improves, reducing heat leakage at the 
evaporator inlet. Furthermore, the mLHP’s performance in -
creases with higher input power at larger tilt angles. The mLHP 
has a maximum dissipating power of 7 W, with an equivalent 
thermal conductivity of 1,831–24,449 W/(m ⋅ K) and a thermal 
resistance of 19.04–1.15 K/W. The mLHP underwent a 30-day 
long-term aging test at a high temperature of 90
◦
C, during which 
no performance degradation was observed.
This mLHP demonstrates potential for high-heat-flux thermal 
management in space-constrained systems, offering an alterna -
tive to conventional HPs and graphene heat spreaders. Howev -
er, commercialization hinges on resolving two critical barriers: (1) 
manufacturing consistency for wick-driven mLHPs demands 
scalable processes ensuring batch reproducibility and precision 
in pore geometry/thickness; and (2) long-term reliability valida -
tion is required under dynamic conditions (thermal, vibration, 
multi-orientation) per industrial standards. While the mLHP in 
this work exhibits some flexibility, as the bending angle in -
creases the supporting spring may puncture the condenser, 
leading to device failure. This highlights a fundamental conflict 
in ultra-thin designs that also require flexibility, whereas existing 
mechanical architectures suffer from reliability deficiencies un -
der repeated bending. Future efforts can explore material inno -
vations to achieve mechanical flexibility, thereby opening trans -
formative pathways for thermal management in next-generation 
flexible electronics. Concurrently, extreme-environment valida -
tion will bridge the gap between laboratory prototypes and 
real-world applications, extending its potential for thermal regu -
lation in space-constrained systems such as spacecraft 
electronics.
METHODS
Device fabrication
The evaporator’s bottom and cover plates are made of high- 
strength brass and have a thickness δ of 0.15 mm. The supple -
mentary wick is placed in a 300-mL solution containing 10 g of 
oxalic acid, heated in a 50
◦
C water bath, and subjected to ul -
trasonic cleaning for 30 min. It is then cleaned with deionized 
water and vacuum dried. The supplementary wick undergoes 
a modification process that enhances its capillary performance. 
Next, the bottom plate of the evaporator, the main wick, and 
the supplementary wick are spot welded together for pre-fixing, 
facilitating subsequent evaporator fabrication. The bottom plate 
and cover plate of the evaporator are then welded together us -
ing a diffusion welding machine. The condenser is made from a 
double-sided copper-plated polyimide film, with an overall 
thickness of 48.5 μ m (including an 18- μ m copper layer, a 
12.5- μ m polyimide layer, and an 18- μ m copper layer). The 
application of a copper layer on the external surface serves 
to enhance barrier properties, while the deposition of a copper 
layer on the internal surface improves solderability. During the 
fabrication process, the condenser is first shaped using a 
cold press machine to the desired form, and the diffusion weld -
ing process, which has been applied in the fabrication of 
certain LHPs and vapor chamber devices,
40–42
is then used 
to weld it together with the evaporator. Notably, due to the 
small thickness of the condenser and to prevent collapse dur -
ing the vacuum process due to insufficient support, a pre- 
shaped spring is added during the welding process as support. 
Additionally, the supplementary wick is designed to be placed 
in the center of the spring.
